## 1. Organizational Climate

**Organizational climate = employees’ shared perception of what the workplace is like.**

Think:

> **Climate = How people experience the workplace TODAY**

It is:

- **Perceptual** — based on how employees experience the workplace.
- **Shared** — not just one person's opinion.
- **Changeable** — can change with leadership, workload, policies and events.

### Example

Two factories have the **same safety policy**.

Factory A:

> Employees freely report hazards.

Factory B:

> Employees are afraid to report hazards.

The written policy is the same, but the **climate is different**.

### Memory

> **Climate = What people EXPERIENCE**

### 2. Responsibility

### 6. Standards

# 3. Measuring Organizational Climate

How do we know what the climate actually is?

The PDF says to **measure employee perceptions and compare them with behavior and HR data**.

Three major methods:

### Surveys

Questionnaires or pulse surveys.

### Interviews

Individual or group conversations.

### Observation

Look at what employees actually do.

Also use HR indicators such as:

- turnover,
- absenteeism,
- incidents.

### Important exam point

> Climate is **not the objective condition itself**.

It is the **shared perception of that condition**.

# 4. Organizational Culture

Culture goes deeper than climate.

The PDF describes culture as:

> **“The way we do things around here.”**

It consists of shared:

- values,
- patterns,
- assumptions,

that guide organizational behavior.

Culture is:

### Deeper

It includes assumptions people rarely question.

### More stable

It develops over time and changes more slowly.

### Learned

New employees learn it through:

- socialization,
- stories,
- role models,
- rewards.

### Example

Suppose everyone in a company believes:

> “Questioning seniors is unacceptable.”

Nobody may have written this as a rule.

But new employees learn it by observing others.

That's **culture**.

### Memory

> **Climate = experience**  
> **Culture = deeper way of doing things**

# 5. Climate vs Culture

This is **very likely for match-the-pairs / explain / comparison questions**.

|Climate|Culture|
|---|---|
|Surface-level perceptions|Deep values and assumptions|
|Changes relatively quickly|Changes more slowly|
|Usually consciously perceived|Often taken for granted|
|How work feels|Why people behave that way|
|Like **today's weather**|Like a **deeper pattern**|

The PDF gives this exact comparison framework.

### Very simple example

A new manager joins.

After one month, employees say:

> “Meetings feel much more open now.”

That's mainly **climate**.

But suppose employees have believed for years:

> “Managers should never be questioned.”

That's **culture**.

### 🔥 Remember this

> **Climate changes faster. Culture changes slower.**

# 1. Schein’s Three Levels of Organizational Culture

This is **very important for an exam**.

Schein says culture has **3 levels**, from visible to deep:

> **Artifacts → Espoused/Adopted Values → Basic Assumptions**

Think of culture like an **iceberg**.

### Level 1 — Artifacts

These are the **visible things**.

Examples:

- dress,
- office design,
- rituals,
- language,
- symbols.

You can easily observe them.

### Example

A company has:

- open offices,
- casual dress,
- startup-style language.

These are **artifacts**.

---

### Level 2 — Espoused / Adopted Values

These are the values that the organization **says it believes in**.

Examples:

- innovation,
- customer focus,
- teamwork,
- integrity.

Example:

Company says:

> "We value innovation."

That's an **espoused value**.

---

### Level 3 — Basic Assumptions

This is the **deepest level**.

These are unconscious or taken-for-granted beliefs about:

- people,
- work,
- risk,
- authority,
- success.

Employees may not even realize they have these assumptions.

Example:

Company repeatedly says:

> "We value employee voice."

But everyone actually believes:

> "Questioning senior managers is dangerous."

That hidden belief is a **basic assumption**.

### Memory

> **A → V → A**

**Artifacts → Values → Assumptions**

Or:

> **SEE → SAY → BELIEVE**

# 2. What Does Organizational Culture Do?

Culture acts like an **informal coordination system**.

The PDF gives four important functions:

### 1. Identity

Culture creates a sense of:

> **"Who are we?"**

Example:

Employees feel proud to belong to a company with a strong engineering culture.

### 2. Commitment

Culture connects employees' behavior with a **shared purpose**.

Instead of:

> "I am doing this just because my boss told me."

The employee thinks:

> "This is consistent with what our organization stands for."

### 3. Sense-making

Culture helps employees understand **ambiguous situations**.

Example:

A crisis happens and there is no exact rule.

Employees use organizational norms to decide:

> "What would people in this organization normally do?"

### 4. Control

Culture guides behavior **without constant supervision**.

Example:

Employees don't need someone watching them all the time because they already understand the expected behavior.

### ⚠️ Possible disadvantage

A strong culture can become:

> **Rigid and resistant to change.**

So:

**Strong culture = consistency**

but sometimes:

**Strong culture → rigidity → resistance to change**

# 4. Hofstede’s Cultural Dimensions

Hofstede's framework is a **national-culture lens**, particularly useful for understanding cross-cultural teamwork. The PDF specifically warns not to treat it as a label for every individual.

Your PDF lists four dimensions:

### 1. Power Distance

Question:

> **How comfortable are people with unequal power?**

High power distance:

> "Senior people should have more authority."

Low power distance:

> "Employees can easily question managers."

---

### 2. Individualism vs Collectivism

Question:

> **Do people prioritize personal goals or group loyalty?**

**Individualism:**

> "My individual achievement matters."

**Collectivism:**

> "Group goals and relationships matter."

---

### 3. Uncertainty Avoidance

Question:

> **How comfortable are people with ambiguity and risk?**

High uncertainty avoidance:

> Prefer clear rules and predictability.

Lower uncertainty avoidance:

> More comfortable experimenting with uncertainty.

---

### 4. Long-Term vs Short-Term Orientation

Question:

> **Future persistence or immediate results?**

Long-term orientation emphasizes:

- future benefits,
- persistence,
- long-term planning.

Short-term orientation emphasizes:

- immediate outcomes,
- current expectations/results.

### Memory

> **P-I-U-L**

**P**ower distance  
**I**ndividualism  
**U**ncertainty avoidance  
**L**ong/short-term orientation

# 5. How Organizational Culture Is Formed

The PDF says culture develops from **repeated solutions that become "the way we do things."**

There are four important influences:

### 1. Founder / Early Leaders

Early leaders leave the first cultural imprint through:

- values,
- priorities,
- early decisions.

Example:

A founder strongly values customer service.

Over time:

> "Customer always comes first"

becomes part of the culture.

---

### 2. Industry Demands

Different industries create different norms.

For example:

A safety-critical industry needs strong safety procedures.

A highly innovative technology industry may encourage experimentation.

---

### 3. Critical Events

How leaders respond during important events teaches employees what is really valued.

Example:

During a crisis:

- Leader hides the problem → employees learn to hide problems.
- Leader encourages reporting → employees learn openness.

---

### 4. What Gets Rewarded

Employees notice:

> **"What behavior actually gets rewarded here?"**

If employees who innovate get promoted:

> Innovation becomes part of the culture.

If only short-term sales are rewarded:

> Employees may focus heavily on sales.

### Memory

> **F-I-C-R**

**F**ounders  
**I**ndustry  
**C**ritical events  
**R**ewards

# 1. Organizational Socialization

### Meaning

Organizational socialization is the process by which a **new employee moves from being an outsider to becoming an effective member of the organization**.

Your PDF gives **3 stages**:

> **Anticipatory → Encounter → Metamorphosis**

### 1. Anticipatory Stage

This happens **before joining**.

The employee forms expectations through:

- recruitment,
- interviews,
- placement discussions.

Example:

> "This company has a very friendly and flexible work culture."

That is the employee's expectation before joining.

### 2. Encounter Stage

The employee joins and encounters the **actual workplace reality**.

They learn:

- supervisor behavior,
- team norms,
- actual workload,
- actual culture.

Example:

> Expected: flexible workplace  
> Reality: strict working hours.

This is where the expectation-reality gap appears.

### 3. Metamorphosis Stage

The employee adjusts to the organization.

They develop:

- confidence,
- role clarity,
- effective contribution.

Eventually:

> **Outsider → Effective member**

### Memory

> **A → E → M**

**Anticipatory → Encounter → Metamorphosis**

# 2. How Culture Spreads

The PDF's key idea is:

> **People learn culture more from repeated signals than from posters.**

Four major mechanisms are given:

### 1. Stories

Employees hear stories about:

- founders,
- successes,
- failures,
- crises.

These stories communicate:

> "What is valued here?"

### 2. Rituals & Symbols

Examples:

- onboarding,
- town halls,
- ceremonies,
- office spaces.

These repeatedly reinforce cultural values.

### 3. Language

Organizations develop shared:

- jargon,
- phrases,
- terminology.

This creates a sense of belonging.

### 4. Role Modelling

Employees observe:

> **What leaders and senior employees actually do.**

Example:

Company says:

> "We value safety."

But supervisors ignore safety rules to meet targets.

Employees will learn the **real culture from the supervisor's behavior**, not the poster.

### Memory

> **S-R-L-R**

**Stories – Rituals – Language – Role modelling**

# 3. Person–Organization Fit vs Person–Job Fit

This is a **very good comparison question**.

## Person–Organization Fit (P–O Fit)

Question:

> **"Do my values and preferred way of working fit the organization's culture?"**

Example:

You prefer:

- teamwork,
- open communication,
- experimentation.

You join an organization that values exactly these things.

→ **High P–O Fit**

The PDF links high P–O fit with greater satisfaction and commitment.

---

## Person–Job Fit (P–J Fit)

Question:

> **"Do my abilities, interests and expectations fit the actual job?"**

Example:

You love the company's collaborative culture.

But your actual job is:

> 80% repetitive documentation.

You may have:

✅ Good P–O Fit  
❌ Poor P–J Fit

This exact type of example appears in the PDF.

### 🔥 Difference

> **P–O Fit = Me ↔ Organization**

> **P–J Fit = Me ↔ Job**

# 4. When Culture Becomes a Barrier

This is an important application-based concept.

A culture that helped an organization succeed in the past can later **prevent adaptation**.

### How?

Suppose an organization has a deep assumption:

> "Successful engineers work very fast."

This may have worked previously.

But later the industry requires:

> careful experimentation + reflection + new technology.

The old assumption now becomes a problem.

### What reinforces the old culture?

The PDF points to:

- rewards,
- promotions,
- policies,
- routines,
- leaders.

So:

**Old assumption**  
↓  
**Systems reinforce it**  
↓  
**People defend familiar practices**  
↓  
**Change becomes difficult**

### PDF example

The PDF uses **Nokia** to illustrate how a once-successful engineering orientation can become a barrier during major technology shifts.

### Key exam sentence

> **A strong culture can be both an advantage and a barrier: it creates consistency, but deeply held assumptions may make adaptation difficult when the environment changes.**

# 5. Changing Culture: From Slogans to Systems

This is basically asking:

> **How do we actually change organizational culture?**

The PDF gives **4 steps**:

> **Diagnose → Align → Communicate → Reinforce**

## Step 1 — Diagnose

First understand:

> **What is the current culture/climate? What gap matters?**

Example:

Employees are not reporting safety problems.

Find out why.

---

## Step 2 — Align

Align:

- leadership,
- structures,
- rewards,
- processes

with the desired culture.

Example:

If you want a **learning culture**, don't punish every reported mistake.

---

## Step 3 — Communicate

Explain:

> **Why is the cultural change necessary?**

And communicate consistently.

---

## Step 4 — Reinforce

Reward the desired behavior and socialize new employees into it.

Example:

If you want employees to report safety problems:

✅ reward reporting and learning  
❌ don't reward hiding problems

### Memory

> **D-A-C-R**

**Diagnose → Align → Communicate → Reinforce**

# 1. Safety Climate

Safety climate is about:

> **What the organization really protects when output and safety conflict.**

The important distinction is between **strong** and **weak** safety climate.

### Strong Safety Climate

Management genuinely supports safe work.

Employees feel they can:

- stop unsafe work,
- raise safety concerns,
- report near-misses.

Near-misses are discussed and used for learning.

### Weak Safety Climate

Production targets dominate.

Employees may think:

> "Don't stop the work; we need to meet today's target."

Consequently:

- stopping work is discouraged,
- near-misses are not reported.

### Example

A junior engineer notices a dangerous condition 10 minutes before dispatch.

**Strong climate:**

> "STOP. Let's fix it."

**Weak climate:**

> "We are already late. Just continue."

### Exam point

> A strong safety climate encourages reporting, speaking up and safe behavior; a weak safety climate allows production pressure to suppress safety concerns.

# 2. Quality & Learning Culture

The PDF compares **blame culture** with **learning culture**.

## Blame Culture

The question is:

> **"Who caused the error?"**

Focus:

- person,
- fault,
- punishment.

Problem:

Employees may start **hiding errors**.

### Example

A production defect occurs.

Manager says:

> "Whose mistake was this?"

The employee becomes afraid and may hide future mistakes.

---

## Learning Culture

The question becomes:

> **"How did the system allow this?"**

Focus:

- prevention,
- process,
- system weaknesses,
- learning.

The error becomes an opportunity to improve the system.

### Example

Instead of blaming the engineer:

> "Why did the defect pass through testing?"

The team examines:

- review process,
- test coverage,
- communication,
- checkpoints.

### 🔥 Remember

> **Blame culture = Who?**  
> **Learning culture = How?**

This is an excellent distinction for an application question.

# 4. “STOP THE LINE?” Group Case

This is an **application of climate and culture**.

### Situation

A manufacturing plant has:

- excellent written safety policy,
- aggressive production targets,
- supervisors who dislike stoppages,
- very few reported near-misses.

Notice the contradiction:

**Formal policy says:**

> Safety is important.

**Actual behavior says:**

> Production is more important.

This tells us there is a gap between:

> **Formal policy ↔ Lived climate**

### How to answer the case

The PDF tells you to use:

> **Concept → Evidence → Diagnosis → Action**

### Step 1: Evidence

For example:

- Supervisors dislike stoppages.
- Near-misses are rarely reported.

### Step 2: Diagnosis

Likely:

> **Weak safety climate**

because employees appear to experience pressure against stopping production or reporting problems.

### Step 3: Behavior being reinforced

The system is effectively reinforcing:

> **"Keep production moving and don't report problems."**

### Step 4: Action

Two sensible actions:

> Make stopping unsafe work explicitly supported by leadership.

and

> Reward reporting and learning from near-misses rather than only rewarding production output.

That follows the PDF's diagnostic approach.

# 5. Mini Climate & Culture Audit

The PDF turns all these concepts into a practical **diagnostic tool**.

You examine four areas:

### Leadership

> **Do leaders model what they expect?**

Example:

Leaders say safety matters, but themselves bypass safety procedures.

→ problem.

### Communication

> **Can information and bad news move upward?**

Can an employee tell the manager:

> "There is a serious problem"?

### Safety / Quality

> **What gets protected when targets conflict?**

If production target and safety conflict, which one actually wins?

### Voice / Fairness

> **Can people question, disagree and report?**

If employees cannot challenge decisions, the climate may discourage speaking up.

---

## The Audit Method

The PDF gives a **5-step diagnostic ladder**:

> **1. What happened?**  
> **2. What evidence do we have?**  
> **3. Climate, culture, or both?**  
> **4. What behaviour is being reinforced?**  
> **5. What should change — behaviour + system?**

This is **very useful for long-answer case questions**.

# 🔥 Example: How to Apply It

Suppose employees never report software defects because managers publicly blame whoever made the mistake.

### What happened?

Defects are being hidden.

### Evidence?

Employees avoid reporting mistakes.

### Climate/culture?

Likely a **blame-oriented climate**, potentially supported by deeper cultural assumptions.

### What behavior is reinforced?

> **Hide mistakes rather than report them.**

### What should change?

- Leaders should focus on prevention.
- Reporting problems should be encouraged.
- Review systems should examine process failures.
