# Cognitive Bias

Your PDF introduces **Heuristics** first and then defines **Cognitive Bias**.

## What is Cognitive Bias?

A **cognitive bias** is a **systematic tendency of our brain to think or judge in a way that can deviate from ideal or completely rational reasoning**.

Your PDF describes it as the:

> **gap between how we ought to reason ideally and how our brain actually reasons.**

The important thing is:

> **Bias is a normal function of the human brain.**

We **cannot completely avoid biases**, but we **can manage them**.

# System 1 and System 2

Think of your brain as having **two different ways of thinking**.

### System 1 — Fast thinking

System 1 is:

**Fast + automatic + intuitive**

It gives you an answer **without much conscious effort**.

For example:

You see:

**2 + 2 = ?**

Your brain immediately says:

**4**

You don't consciously perform a detailed calculation. The answer just appears.

Another example from your PDF:

An experienced mechanic hears a machine for a couple of seconds and says:

> “Misalignment.”

That is most likely **System 1 pattern recognition**.

The mechanic has seen similar situations many times, so the brain recognizes the pattern very quickly.

---

# System 2 — Slow thinking

System 2 is:

**Slow + deliberate + analytical**

Here, you consciously think through the problem.

For example:

**24 × 17 = ?**

You probably don't instantly know the answer.

You start doing:

That deliberate calculation is **System 2**.

Your PDF specifically gives an example where System 2 means identifying assumptions, checking calculations and comparing evidence rather than accepting a confident answer immediately

--- 
# 1. Confirmation Bias

## What is it?

**Confirmation bias** is the tendency to:

> **look for, notice, and give more importance to information that supports what you already believe, while ignoring or downplaying information that contradicts it.**

Your PDF gives the example of scientists selectively reporting results that support their hypothesis while overlooking conflicting evidence.

### In very simple words

You already have an answer in your head:

> **“I think X is true.”**

Then instead of asking:

> “What does the evidence actually say?”

your brain starts asking:

> “What evidence can I find that proves I'm right?”

That's **confirmation bias**.

---
# How to reduce / Debias Confirmation Bias

This part is **very important for your exam**, because the PDF asks not only to identify bias but also to explain how to debias it.

Your PDF says:

### 1. Seek contradictory information

Don't only search for evidence that supports you.

Ask:

> **“What evidence would prove me wrong?”**

### 2. Get constructive criticism

Discuss your idea with people who have **different perspectives**.

Your PDF specifically says to choose colleagues with different perspectives rather than only people who agree with you.

### 3. Ask appropriate questions

While gathering information, don't ask only:

> “How can I prove I'm right?”

Ask:

> “What other explanations are possible?”

### 4. Decide based on evidence

After considering both supporting and contradictory information, make the decision.

# 2. Framing Bias

## What is Framing Bias?

**Framing bias** happens when the **same information is presented in different ways**, and that presentation changes how we feel about it or how we judge it.

In simple words:

> **Same facts + different wording → different decision**

Your PDF literally demonstrates this idea with the example of **99% fat-free** versus **1% fat**. Both describe the same thing, but one sounds more attractive.

---

## Great example

Imagine a company has this fact:

> **80% of employees stay for at least one year.**

Now look at two ways of presenting exactly the same information.

### Frame A — Negative

> “20% of employees leave this company within the first year.”

You may think:

> “Damn, 20% leave. That's high.”

### Frame B — Positive

> “80% of employees stay with this company for at least one year.”

Now you may think:

> “That's actually pretty good.”

But:

**20% leave = 80% stay**

The underlying information has **not changed**.

Only the **presentation** changed.

Your emotional reaction and judgment changed.

That is **Framing Bias**.

---

# Why does framing affect us?

Our brain doesn't process only raw numbers.

The **wording and presentation** can influence:

- emotions
    
- perception of risk
    
- attractiveness of an option
    
- judgment
    
- final decision
    

So when someone frames information positively or negatively, they can influence how you perceive the exact same facts.

---

# Engineering example

Suppose an engineering project has a **90% success rate**.

One manager says:

> “This project has a 90% chance of success.”

You feel confident.

Another manager says:

> “This project has a 10% chance of failure.”

You may become worried.

But mathematically:

**90% success = 10% failure**

Same information.

Different frame.

---
# How to identify Framing Bias

Ask yourself:

> **“Did the facts change, or did only the way they were presented change?”**

If:

**Facts same ✅**  
**Presentation different ✅**  
**Judgment changes ✅**

→ **Framing Bias**

---

# How to reduce Framing Bias

Your PDF gives a very useful 4-step process.

### 1. Recognize

Ask:

> **“Am I reacting to the wording rather than the actual information?”**

### 2. Reframe

Convert the statement into another form.

For example:

**20% leave → 80% stay**

This helps you see the same information from another perspective.

### 3. Check the facts

Don't decide based only on the wording.

Look at things such as:

- salary
    
- growth
    
- work culture
    
- location
    
- job security
    

Your PDF specifically mentions considering these factors in the placement example.

### 4. Decide using complete evidence

Make the decision based on the **actual information**, not on which version sounds better.


# Anchoring Bias

This is the **third cognitive bias** in your PDF.

## What is Anchoring Bias?

**Anchoring bias** is the tendency to rely too heavily on the **first piece of information** we receive when making a later judgment or decision.

That first information becomes the **“anchor”**, and it influences how we evaluate everything afterward.

### Simple idea

> **First information → becomes reference point → influences later judgment**

---

## Great example: Laptop price

Suppose you see a laptop:

> **Original price: ₹80,000**

Then the seller says:

> **Today: ₹55,000**

Immediately you think:

> “Wow! ₹55,000 is a great deal!”

But why?

Because **₹80,000 became your anchor**.

You are comparing ₹55,000 against ₹80,000 instead of asking:

> “What is this laptop actually worth?”

Your PDF gives this exact example.

Maybe the laptop is actually worth only ₹45,000.

Then ₹55,000 isn't necessarily a great deal.

---

# Another example: Salary negotiation

Suppose an interviewer says:

> “The salary for this position is ₹12 LPA.”

That **₹12 LPA becomes your anchor**.

Later they offer:

> ₹10 LPA

You may think:

> “That's low.”

But imagine they had originally said:

> “The salary is ₹8 LPA.”

Then ₹10 LPA might feel excellent.

The actual offer is still **₹10 LPA**, but your perception changed because of the initial number.

---

# Engineering example

Suppose an engineer's first estimate for a project is:

> **₹50 lakh**

Later, after detailed analysis, the realistic cost appears to be:

> **₹70 lakh**

The engineer may still keep thinking:

> “₹70 lakh feels too high.”

Why?

Because **₹50 lakh became the initial reference point**.

That is anchoring.

---

# Types of anchors mentioned in your PDF

Your PDF gives several examples of anchors:

### Life anchors

Your first impression or previous relationship with:

- friends
    
- colleagues
    
- people
    

can influence later judgments.

### Buying something

The **initial price** can become an anchor.

### Rating something

A **previous rating or previous incident** can influence your current judgment.

---

# The most important thing to understand

Anchoring does **not** mean the first information is necessarily correct.

It means:

> **Even when better information appears later, the initial information continues to influence your judgment.**

That's the key.

---

# How to reduce Anchoring Bias

Your PDF gives several approaches.

### 1. Make an independent estimate

Before seeing the offer/initial number, determine what you think the reasonable value should be.

For example:

> “Before seeing the seller's price, I estimate this laptop is worth ₹50,000.”

Then compare.

### 2. Compare multiple alternatives

Don't rely on one initial number.

Check:

**₹55k → ₹60k → ₹48k → ₹52k**

Now you have a broader reference.

### 3. Use objective criteria

For performance evaluation, don't let the first impression determine everything.

Look at actual evidence.

### 4. Generate alternatives

In decision-making, your PDF specifically suggests generating alternatives **before evaluating the first suggestion**.

# 4. Sunk Cost Fallacy

This is the **4th bias** in your PDF, after Anchoring Bias.

## What is Sunk Cost Fallacy?

**Sunk cost fallacy** is the tendency to **continue with a decision because you have already invested time, money, effort, or resources in it**, even when new evidence shows that continuing is no longer the best choice.

Your PDF also connects it with **commitment bias**: continuing to support a past decision despite new evidence suggesting it is not the best course of action.

### The key idea

> **“I have already invested so much, so I cannot stop now.”**

That's the trap.

---

# Great example: ₹50,000 course

Imagine you paid:

**₹50,000** for a course.

After one month, you realize:

> “This course is actually useless for me.”

You have two choices:

### Choice A

Continue attending because:

> “But I've already paid ₹50,000.”

### Choice B

Stop and use your remaining time for something more useful.

The ₹50,000 is **already gone**.

Whether you continue or stop **does not bring that money back**.

So the rational question is:

> **“From this point forward, what is the better choice?”**

Not:

> “How much have I already spent?”

Your PDF gives essentially this exact example.

---

# Movie example

Your PDF asks:

> You went for a movie. At the interval, you feel that it is boring. What will you do?

Suppose you paid ₹300.

After 1 hour:

> “This movie is terrible.”

You might think:

> “I paid ₹300, so I have to finish it.”

That's **sunk cost fallacy**.

The ₹300 is already spent.

You should decide based on:

> **“Will the remaining 1 hour be worth watching?”**

---

# Food example

Suppose you order a large pizza for ₹800.

After eating two slices, you are completely full.

You think:

> “I paid ₹800, so I must finish it.”

That's another example from the same principle.

The money is already spent. Eating more doesn't recover it.

---

# Engineering example

Suppose a company has already spent:

**₹20 crore + 2 years**

on developing a product.

New testing shows:

> The technology is unlikely to work commercially.

Management says:

> “We've already spent ₹20 crore. We can't abandon it now.”

So they spend another ₹10 crore.

This could be a **sunk cost fallacy**.

The correct question is:

> **“Is spending the next ₹10 crore worthwhile given what we know now?”**

Not:

> “We've already spent ₹20 crore, so we must continue.”

---

# Why does this happen?

The thinking looks like:

---

# Very important distinction

### Rational thinking

> “The past cost is gone. What should I do now?”

### Sunk Cost Fallacy

> “I've already invested so much. I have to continue.”

---

# How to avoid it

Ask yourself:

> **“If I had not already invested anything, would I choose this option today?”**

That's a powerful test.

For example:

You paid ₹50,000 for a course.

Imagine someone offered you the same course **today for free**.

Would you still choose to spend your next 3 months on it?

If the answer is **no**, the previous ₹50,000 should not force you to continue.

# 5. Availability Bias

This is the **5th bias** in your PDF.

## What is Availability Bias?

**Availability bias** is the tendency to judge how likely or important something is based on **how easily examples of it come to mind**.

Your PDF specifically says that the information available in memory tends to be influenced by **vivid, unusual, or emotionally charged examples**.

### In very simple words

> **“If I can remember it easily, I feel it must be common or likely.”**

But **easy to remember ≠ actually likely**.

---

## Great example

Imagine you recently watch several news reports about **plane crashes**.

After that, someone asks:

> “Is flying dangerous?”

You might think:

> “Yes, plane crashes happen quite often!”

Why?

Because those crashes are **fresh and vivid in your memory**.

But you haven't checked the actual statistics.

Your brain is estimating probability based on **what is easily available in memory**.

That's availability bias.

---

# Engineering example from your PDF

An engineer recently witnesses a **battery overheating incident** in the laboratory.

Later, while evaluating a new battery design, the engineer immediately thinks:

> “Overheating is probably the biggest failure risk.”

But historical data shows that **electrical connection failure is actually much more common**.

The engineer's recent, memorable experience is dominating their judgment.

That's **Availability Bias**.

---

# Student example — VERY relevant for your exam

Suppose you have studied:

**Binary Search, Heap and Graphs**

very recently.

And you've seen Binary Search questions in several mock tests.

You start thinking:

> “Binary Search is probably one of the most important topics.”

But perhaps another topic is equally important or even more likely to appear.

Your judgment is being influenced by what is **most easily available in your memory**.

Your PDF gives this exact kind of student application: students may overestimate recently studied topics or topics frequently appearing in mock tests while underestimating less common but still important topics.

---

# Medical example

Suppose you recently hear a story about someone suffering from a rare disease.

Later, you experience a common symptom.

You think:

> “Maybe I have that rare disease too!”

The story is **easy to remember**, so your brain may overestimate how likely that disease is.

The PDF gives this as a medical application and recommends considering **prevalence and base rates**.

---

# Business example

Suppose you hear a memorable story:

> “My friend's investment made 10× returns.”

You may start thinking:

> “This investment strategy must be excellent.”

But one memorable success doesn't tell you how often that strategy succeeds overall.

The PDF recommends using **historical data and multiple sources rather than memorable examples**.

---

# Availability vs Confirmation Bias

Don't confuse these.

### Confirmation Bias

> “I already believe X, so I look for information supporting X.”

### Availability Bias

> “This example comes easily to mind, so I think it is more likely.”

Example:

**Confirmation:**  
“I think this machine has a bearing problem, so I look for evidence supporting bearing failure.”

**Availability:**  
“I recently saw a bearing failure, so I think bearing failure is probably the most likely problem.”

### 1. Ask yourself

> **“Am I judging based on what I remember easily, or on actual data?”**

### 2. Check base rates

### 3. Use historical records

Don't rely on one memorable incident.

### 4. Consider multiple possibilities

For an engineering failure, don't focus on only the failure mode you recently experienced.

# Survivorship Bias

This is the **6th bias** in your PDF, after Availability Bias.

## What is Survivorship Bias?

**Survivorship bias** is the tendency to focus on the people, companies, products, or strategies that **survived or succeeded**, while ignoring those that **failed or disappeared**.

Your PDF defines it as concentrating on those that “survived” a process and ignoring those that didn't, which can make a strategy look more effective than it really is.

### In very simple words

> **“I only look at the winners, so I think their method must be the reason for success.”**

But you haven't looked at the **losers**.

---

## Great example: Successful entrepreneurs

Imagine you read about 10 successful startup founders.

You notice:

- They didn't sleep much.
    
- They took huge risks.
    
- They dropped out of college.
    
- They worked 16 hours a day.
    

You conclude:

> “To become successful, I should drop out and work 16 hours a day.”

But what about the **thousands of people who did exactly those things and still failed?**

You didn't study them.

That's **survivorship bias**.

You are looking only at the **survivors/winners**.

---

## Lottery example

Suppose you read stories about people who won the lottery.

You might conclude:

> “Buying lottery tickets works! Look at all these winners.”

But you aren't focusing on the **millions of people who bought tickets and lost**.

The winners are much more visible because they **survived the process as winners**.

Your PDF specifically uses **lottery winners** as an example.

---

## Engineering example

Suppose a company studies 5 successful machines and finds:

> “All these machines used Technology X.”

Management concludes:

> “Technology X is definitely the reason they succeeded.”

But what if **50 other projects also used Technology X and failed?**

Ignoring those failures creates survivorship bias.

A proper analysis should examine:

**Successful cases + unsuccessful cases**

not just the successful ones.

---

# Difference from Availability Bias

These two can be confusing.

### Availability Bias

> “This example is easy to remember, so I think it is common or likely.”

### Survivorship Bias

> “I am only looking at the cases that succeeded and ignoring the failures.”

For example:

**Availability:**  
“I remember a famous startup that became successful.”

**Survivorship:**  
“I study only successful startups and ignore failed startups.”

---

# How to reduce Survivorship Bias

Your PDF gives the key recommendation:

### Don't blindly copy success stories

Ask:

> **“What about the people who tried the same thing and failed?”**

### Look at the complete dataset

Include:

**Winners + Losers**

### Customize the approach

The PDF specifically says:

> **Avoid blindly copying success stories** and **customize the approach**.

# Status Quo Bias

## What is Status Quo Bias?

**Status quo bias** is the tendency to prefer **keeping the current situation unchanged** rather than choosing a new alternative.

Your PDF describes it as:

> **A preference for the maintenance of one's existing state of affairs.**

### In simple words

> **“Let's keep doing what we're already doing.”**

Even when a better alternative exists.

---

# Great example

Suppose your team has been using an old software tool for 5 years.

A new tool is:

- faster
    
- safer
    
- more efficient
    

But the team says:

> **“We've always used the old one. Let's continue with it.”**

They aren't necessarily choosing it because it is better.

They are choosing it because **it is the current option**.

That's **Status Quo Bias**.

Your PDF gives essentially this exact engineering example.

---

# Engineering examples

### 1. Engineering Design

An engineer continues using a traditional design even though **better alternatives** are available.

### 2. Work Processes

Employees continue following an inefficient procedure because they are already accustomed to it.

### 3. Technology

A company avoids adopting new technology because changing the existing system feels **risky or inconvenient**.

Changing something involves:

**uncertainty + effort + learning + risk**

So the current option feels:

> **familiar + comfortable + safe**

Even if the new option may objectively be better.

The PDF also notes that status quo bias can intersect with other non-rational processes such as **loss aversion**.

---

# How to identify it

Ask:

> **“Am I choosing this because it is genuinely better, or simply because it is what we already have?”**

That's the key question.

---

# Halo Effect and Horn Effect

Your PDF presents **Halo and Horn Effect** together as the 8th bias.

Both are about **letting one characteristic of a person influence your overall judgment of that person**.

---

## Halo Effect

### What is it?

**Halo effect** occurs when **one positive quality** of a person influences our overall judgment of them.

Your PDF gives this example:

> A student speaks confidently, so the teacher assumes the student is also highly competent.

### Example

Imagine an interview candidate:

- speaks very confidently ✅
- dresses professionally ✅
- communicates well ✅

You immediately think:

> “This person must be an excellent programmer.”

But you haven't actually tested their programming ability.

You allowed **one or a few positive impressions** to influence your judgment of their other qualities.

That's **Halo Effect**.

### Memory

> **Halo = one good quality makes the whole person look good.**

---

# Horn Effect

### What is it?

**Horn effect** is the opposite.

It occurs when **one negative quality** influences our overall judgment of a person.

### Example

Suppose an employee arrives late **once**.

The manager thinks:

> “He's unreliable and probably incompetent.”

But being late once doesn't prove that the employee is incompetent.

One negative behaviour has influenced the manager's entire judgment.

That's **Horn Effect**.

### Memory

> **Horn = one bad quality makes the whole person look bad.**

---

# Very easy comparison

|Halo Effect|Horn Effect|
|---|---|
|Positive impression|Negative impression|
|One good quality affects overall judgment|One bad quality affects overall judgment|
|**“He's good at this, so he's good at everything.”**|**“He's bad at this, so he's bad at everything.”**|

### Think of it like this:

---

# Engineering / workplace example

Suppose you're evaluating two engineers.

### Engineer A

Very confident during the presentation.

You think:

> “Excellent engineer.”

But you haven't checked their technical work.

→ **Halo Effect**

### Engineer B

Makes one mistake during the presentation.

You think:

> “This engineer is poor.”

Even though their actual design is excellent.

→ **Horn Effect**

---

# How to reduce Halo & Horn Effects

This is important because your PDF gives specific debiasing methods.

### 1. Separate the dimensions

Evaluate each quality **independently**.

For example:

Don't let communication automatically determine technical skill.

### 2. Use evidence

Base your evaluation on:

- specific behaviours
- performance data
- multiple observations

rather than general impressions.

### 3. Delay the overall judgment

First evaluate individual characteristics.

**Then** form your overall opinion.

# Bandwagon Effect

This is the **9th bias** in your PDF, after Halo/Horn Effect. The PDF defines it as a psychological phenomenon where people do something **because others are doing it**.

## What is Bandwagon Effect?

**Bandwagon effect** is the tendency to adopt a belief, decision, product, behaviour, or opinion mainly because **many other people are doing it**.

### In simple words

> **“Everyone is doing it, so it must be right.”**

You follow the **crowd** instead of independently evaluating the decision.

---

## Great example

Suppose your entire class is preparing for an exam using a particular book.

Everyone says:

> “This is the best book!”

You haven't checked other books, but you immediately decide:

> “Okay, I'll use this one too.”

Why?

Not because you evaluated its quality yourself.

Because **everyone else is using it**.

That's **Bandwagon Effect**.

---

# Social media example

You see a reel saying:

> “Everyone is buying this phone!”

It has **2 million views**.

You immediately think:

> “This phone must be amazing.”

You may buy it without checking:

- specifications
    
- price
    
- alternatives
    
- actual reviews
    
- whether it suits your needs
    

The popularity itself influenced your decision.

---

# Engineering example

Suppose most engineers in your organization use a particular software tool.

A new engineer asks:

> “Why are we using this tool?”

Someone replies:

> “Everyone uses it.”

The engineer accepts it without checking whether another tool would actually be better.

That is a **bandwagon effect**.

The decision is based on **what the majority does**, rather than evidence.

---

# Why does it happen?

Because following the majority can feel:

**safe + socially accepted + less risky**

You may think:

> “If so many people believe it, they probably know something I don't.”

But the majority can also be wrong.

---

# Bandwagon vs Confirmation Bias

These are easy to confuse.

### Confirmation Bias

> **“I already believe X, so I look for evidence supporting X.”**

### Bandwagon Effect

> **“Everyone believes X, so I also believe X.”**

Example:

**Confirmation:**  
“I think this company is good, so I search for positive reviews.”

**Bandwagon:**  
“Everyone says this company is good, so I assume it's good.”

---

# How to reduce Bandwagon Effect

Your PDF's application asks you to reflect:

> **“Am I just a part of the herd?”**

So before following the crowd, ask:

### 1. Why do I believe this?

Is it because of **evidence** or because everyone else believes it?

### 2. What are the alternatives?

Look at other options instead of automatically choosing the popular one.

### 3. Make an independent evaluation

Check the facts yourself.

# Negativity Bias

This is the **10th bias** in your PDF, after the Bandwagon Effect.

## What is Negativity Bias?

**Negativity bias** is the tendency to give **more attention, importance, or weight to negative information than to equally significant positive information**.

### In simple words

> **“One bad thing affects me more than many good things.”**

---

## Great example

Suppose your professor gives you feedback on a project:

- ✅ 9 positive comments
    
- ❌ 1 critical comment
    

You come home and keep thinking:

> **“That one thing was wrong. Maybe my whole project is bad.”**

You completely forget the 9 positive comments.

That's **Negativity Bias**.

Your PDF gives essentially this exact engineering example.

---

# Engineering example

Imagine you present a design to your team.

You receive:

> 9 people: “The design is good.”  
> 1 person: “I think there is a problem with the cooling system.”

Instead of evaluating all the evidence, you become obsessed with that one criticism.

You start thinking:

> “Maybe the entire design is wrong.”

The **negative information receives disproportionate weight**.

That's negativity bias.

---

# Why is it dangerous?

Because it can distort your evaluation.

You may have:

**90% positive evidence**

and

**10% negative evidence**

but your mind may behave as though:

**90% doesn't matter → 10% matters enormously**

This can lead to:

- unnecessary stress
    
- poor self-evaluation
    
- overly negative decisions
    
- ignoring successful aspects of a situation
    

---

# How to reduce Negativity Bias

Your PDF gives **three methods**.

### 1. Balance the evidence

Deliberately identify **both positive and negative information**.

Ask:

> “What went well AND what went wrong?”

### 2. Check proportions

Ask:

> **“Is this one negative event representative of the overall pattern?”**

For example:

9 positive reviews + 1 negative review

doesn't necessarily mean:

> “Everything is bad.”

### 3. Use objective data

Base the decision on the **complete evidence**, not whichever information creates the strongest emotional reaction.

---

# Negativity Bias vs Halo/Horn Effect

### Negativity Bias

One or more **negative pieces of information receive too much attention/weight**.

### Horn Effect

One **negative quality of a person** influences your overall judgment of that person.

Example:

**Negativity bias:**  
“My project has 9 good comments and 1 bad comment, but I keep focusing on the bad one.”

**Horn effect:**  
“This student made one mistake, so I think the student is generally incompetent.”
