## What is Change Management?

Your PDF defines it as a **structured approach to shifting individuals, teams and organizations** from their **current state to a desired state**. It operates at strategic, managerial and operational levels and concerns:

> **People + Processes + Performance**

## Planned vs Unplanned Change

### Planned Change

Change that is deliberately introduced.

Examples from your PDF:

- implementing new software,
- restructuring,
- mergers,
- changing marketing strategies,
- adopting digital technologies.

### Unplanned Change

Change caused by unexpected events.

Examples:

- economic crisis,
- natural disaster,
- sudden regulatory change,
- unexpected employee departure,
- sudden market changes.

### Easy distinction

> **Planned = “We decided to change.”**

> **Unplanned = “We had to change.”**

# 1. Lewin’s Change Management Model

Lewin's model explains organizational change in **3 simple stages**:

### **Unfreeze → Change → Refreeze**

### 1. Unfreeze

Prepare people for change.

The organization makes employees understand:

- Why the current situation is not sufficient.
    
- Why change is necessary.
    
- What problems will occur if nothing changes.
    

**Example:**  
A company is using an old manual billing system. Management explains that it causes delays and errors and announces that a digital billing system will be introduced.

### 2. Change

Actual transition happens.

People:

- learn new methods,
    
- adopt new technology,
    
- change their behavior,
    
- experiment with the new system.
    

**Example:**  
Employees are trained to use the new billing software.

### 3. Refreeze

The new way becomes the **normal way of working**.

The organization:

- standardizes the new process,
    
- creates policies,
    
- provides rewards/support,
    
- ensures employees continue using it.
    

**Example:**  
The company stops accepting manual billing and makes digital billing the standard process.

### Easy memory

**U-C-R**

> **U = Prepare**  
> **C = Implement**  
> **R = Stabilize**

### Exam answer

> **Lewin's Change Management Model** consists of three stages: **Unfreeze, Change and Refreeze**. Unfreeze prepares employees for change, Change involves implementing the new methods, and Refreeze stabilizes the new practices so that they become the normal way of working.

# 2. Force-Field Analysis

This is associated with **Kurt Lewin**.

The basic idea is:

> **Some forces push for change, while some forces resist change.**

The organization has to examine both and shift the balance toward the desired change.

### Driving Forces

These **support change**.

Examples:

- New technology
    
- Leadership support
    
- Competitive pressure
    
- Customer demand
    

### Restraining Forces

These **oppose change**.

Examples:

- Employee resistance
    
- Lack of training
    
- Fear of job loss
    
- Lack of resources
    

### Example

Suppose a factory wants to introduce automation.

**Driving forces:**

- Faster production
    
- Lower errors
    
- Leadership support
    

**Restraining forces:**

- Employees fear job loss
    
- Employees lack technical skills
    
- High initial cost
    

Management can:

**Increase driving forces**  
→ show benefits, provide management support

**Decrease restraining forces**  
→ train employees, provide job security/redeployment

This shifts the balance toward change.

### Memory trick

> **Force-Field = FOR change vs AGAINST change**

### Exam point

Don't confuse this with a change stage model.

**Lewin 3-step model:** tells **how change happens**.  
**Force-field analysis:** tells **what is helping or resisting change**.

# 3. Kotter’s 8-Step Change Model

Kotter's model focuses particularly on the **people side of change** and gives **8 structured steps**.

### The 8 steps

### 1. Create Urgency

Make people understand:

> "We need to change NOW."

Example: competition is increasing, so an organization must adopt AI.

### 2. Form a Powerful Coalition

Create a strong group of leaders/champions who support the change.

### 3. Create a Vision for Change

Clearly define:

> "Where are we going?"

Example: "Reduce project delivery time by 25%."

### 4. Communicate the Vision

Explain the vision repeatedly and clearly to employees.

### 5. Remove Obstacles

Identify and eliminate barriers such as:

- lack of training,
    
- outdated procedures,
    
- resistance,
    
- lack of resources.
    

### 6. Create Short-Term Wins

Achieve and show small early successes.

Example:  
First department successfully adopts the new software.

### 7. Build on the Change

Do not stop after the first success. Continue improving and expanding the change.

### 8. Anchor Changes in Culture

Make the new behavior part of the organization's culture.

### Memory trick

**U → C → V → C → O → W → B → A**

Think:

> **Urgency → Coalition → Vision → Communicate → Obstacles → Wins → Build → Anchor**

The exact eight steps are given in the PDF.

# 4. ADKAR Model

This one is **very important for fill-in-the-blanks**.

ADKAR was developed by **Jeff Hiatt**, founder of Prosci, in **1996**.

### ADKAR =

> **A — Awareness**  
> **D — Desire**  
> **K — Knowledge**  
> **A — Ability**  
> **R — Reinforcement**

Let's understand the difference carefully.

### A — Awareness

The person understands:

> **WHY is the change necessary?**

Example: Employees understand why the company must move toward EVs.

### D — Desire

The person develops willingness to support the change.

> **Do I WANT to support it?**

### K — Knowledge

The person learns:

> **HOW do I change?**

Example: Engineers receive training in EV technology and battery systems.

### A — Ability

The person can actually **perform the new behavior**.

Knowledge ≠ Ability.

Someone may know how to use software but still be unable to use it properly in real work.

### R — Reinforcement

Ensure the change continues.

Examples:

- rewards,
    
- recognition,
    
- incentives,
    
- celebrating success.
    

### Tata Motors example from your PDF

For Tata Motors' transition toward EVs:

**Awareness:** urgent need for sustainable vehicles  
**Desire:** employees/stakeholders align with the green vision  
**Knowledge:** engineers trained in EV and battery systems  
**Ability:** practical projects such as Nexon EV development  
**Reinforcement:** milestones, incentives and rewards maintain momentum.

### Best memory

> **Know WHY → WANT → KNOW HOW → CAN DO → KEEP DOING**

That's ADKAR.

# 5. Bridges’ Transition Model

This is the one students often confuse with change-management models.

Bridges focuses on the **psychological transition of people**, not simply the organizational change itself.

The PDF gives three stages:

> **Ending/Loss → Neutral Zone → New Beginning**

### 1. Ending / Loss

People first have to let go of the old situation.

They may feel:

- fear,
    
- sadness,
    
- uncertainty,
    
- resistance.
    

Example:

> "We have always used the old system. Why are they replacing it?"

### 2. Neutral Zone

The old way is gone, but the new way is not yet fully comfortable.

This is the **transition period**.

People may experience:

- confusion,
    
- experimentation,
    
- lower productivity,
    
- uncertainty.
    

Example:

Employees are learning the new software but still making mistakes.

### 3. New Beginning

People finally accept and embrace the new way.

They:

- understand the new role,
    
- gain confidence,
    
- adopt new behaviors,
    
- become committed to the new system.

It has **6 boxes**:

> **Why does the organization exist? What are we trying to achieve?**

Ask:

> **How is the organization arranged?**

Who reports to whom?  
Who has responsibility?

### 3. Relationships

Look at relationships between:

- employees,
    
- teams,
    
- managers.

### 4. Rewards

Ask:

> **What behavior is rewarded?**

Examples:

- salary,
    
- bonuses,
    
- promotions,
    
- recognition.
    

### 5. Leadership

Leadership coordinates the other five areas and gives direction.

### 6. Helpful Mechanisms

These are systems that help employees perform their work.

Examples:

- information systems,
    
- meetings,
    
- procedures,
    
- communication systems,
    
- training systems.

> **P-S-R-R-L-H**

Or remember:

**Purpose → Structure → Relationships → Rewards → Leadership → Helpful mechanisms**

# 2. John Fisher Model

This one is about the **psychological journey people go through during change**.

Your PDF gives **10 stages**.

### 1. Anxiety

> **“Can I cope?”**

The person is uncertain when change is announced.

### 2. Happiness

The person may initially feel optimistic that the change will bring benefits.

### 3. Fear

Now the person starts thinking:

> **“What will happen to me?”**

Example:  
“Will I lose my job?”

### 4. Threat

The change starts becoming real, so insecurity increases.

### 5. Guilt

The person may regret the past or feel uncomfortable about not adapting.

### 6. Depression

The person becomes disengaged.

> “This won't work.”

### 7. Hostility

Active resistance begins.

> “I don't want this change.”

### 8. Denial

The person behaves as though the change is unnecessary or irrelevant.

### 9. Gradual Acceptance

The person begins:

- adapting,
    
- experimenting,
    
- accepting the new situation.
    

### 10. Moving Forward

The person fully engages with the new reality.

### Memory sequence

> **A H F T G D H D A M**

The important idea is:

**Uncertainty → resistance → acceptance → commitment**

# 3. Kübler-Ross Change Curve

Your PDF lists **Kübler-Ross Change Curve (1969)** immediately after Fisher's model.

For exam purposes, remember that this model describes **emotional reactions to change**.

The commonly taught stages are:

> **Denial → Anger → Bargaining → Depression → Acceptance**

### 1. Denial

> “This change isn't really necessary.”

### 2. Anger

> “Why are they doing this?”

### 3. Bargaining

The person tries to negotiate.

> “Can we keep the old system for another year?”

### 4. Depression

The person becomes discouraged or loses motivation.

### 5. Acceptance

The person accepts the reality of the change and starts adapting.

### Example

Company introduces a new software system:

**Denial:** “We don't need it.”  
↓  
**Anger:** “This is making our work difficult.”  
↓  
**Bargaining:** “Can we use both systems?”  
↓  
**Depression:** “I can't learn this.”  
↓  
**Acceptance:** “Okay, I'll learn it.”

### Important distinction

**Fisher:** more detailed psychological journey — **10 stages in your PDF**.  
**Kübler-Ross:** simpler emotional curve — commonly remembered as **5 stages**.

Don't mix them in the exam.

# 4. Principles of Managing the Human Side of Change

This section is extremely important because it explains **how managers should handle people during change**.

Your PDF first gives five key principles:

### 1. Different people react differently

Not everyone responds to change in the same way.

One employee may be excited while another may be scared.

### 2. Everyone has fundamental needs

People want things such as:

- security,
    
- respect,
    
- belonging,
    
- control.
    

Management should consider these needs.

### 3. Change = Loss

People may feel that they are losing:

- familiar routines,
    
- status,
    
- relationships,
    
- job security,
    
- comfort.
    

Therefore, resistance can be a reaction to perceived loss.

### 4. Expectations must be realistic

Don't make unrealistic promises.

For example:

❌ “This new system will solve every problem.”

Better:

✅ “This system will reduce manual work, but employees will need training.”

### 5. Fears must be addressed

Employees' concerns should be listened to and answered.

Example:

Employees fear automation → management provides **training and redeployment opportunities**.

## 1. What is Resistance to Change?

**Resistance to change** means employees **push back against a proposed change**.

Importantly, your PDF says resistance can be:

- **Rational** — based on logical concerns.
    
- **Emotional** — based on fear, insecurity, or feelings.
    
- **Not always negative** — resistance can sometimes provide useful feedback.
    

### Example

A company introduces automation.

An employee says:

> "The new machine will make my current skills useless."

That's resistance, but it may reveal a **real training problem**.

---

# 2. Causes of Resistance to Change

Your PDF divides the causes into **three main categories**.

## A. Economic Factors

Employees may fear:

- **Job loss**
    
- **Demotion**
    
- **Skill mismatch**
    

Example:

> A factory introduces robots → workers fear that their jobs will disappear.

### Memory:

**Economic = Money + Job**

---

## B. Psychological Factors

Your PDF gives:

- **Ego defensiveness**
    
- **Fear of the unknown**
    
- **Low tolerance**
    

### Ego defensiveness

A person feels the change challenges their competence or status.

> "I've been doing this for 15 years. Why should I change?"

### Fear of unknown

People don't know what will happen after the change.

> "Will I be able to handle the new system?"

### Low tolerance

Some people simply find change difficult and prefer stability.

### Memory:

**Psychological = Ego + Unknown + Tolerance**

---

## C. Social & Group Factors

The PDF mentions:

- **Peer conformity**
    
- **Breaking of old networks**
    

### Peer conformity

If everyone's colleagues oppose the change, an individual may also oppose it.

> "Everyone in my department is against it, so I'm against it too."

### Breaking old networks

A change may separate people from:

- old teams,
    
- friendships,
    
- familiar working relationships.

# 3. Cognitive Dissonance

This is another important term.

> **Cognitive dissonance = conflict between existing beliefs and new organizational expectations.**

### Example

An employee believes:

> "Good managers should always supervise their employees directly."

The organization introduces **self-managed teams**.

Now:

**Old belief ≠ New expectation**

This creates psychological discomfort, which can produce resistance.

# 4. Symptoms of Resistance

How can management identify resistance?

Your PDF gives:

### **Absenteeism**

Employees start taking more leave or avoiding work.

### **Low morale**

Motivation and enthusiasm decrease.

### **Sabotage**

Employees deliberately interfere with or damage the change.

### Easy memory:

> **A-M-S = Absenteeism, Morale↓, Sabotage**

# 5. Constructive vs Destructive Resistance

This is **very important for an exam comparison**.

|Constructive Resistance|Destructive Resistance|
|---|---|
|Gives useful feedback|Sabotages change|
|Identifies genuine problems|Deliberately harms implementation|
|Can improve the change|Prevents or damages the change|

Your PDF gives the simplest distinction:

> **Constructive = feedback**  
> **Destructive = sabotage**

### Example

**Constructive:**

> "The software is good, but employees need two weeks of training."

**Destructive:**

> Employee intentionally deletes configuration files so the new system fails.

# 6. Strategies to Manage Resistance

Your PDF gives **three core strategies**.

## A. Communication Strategy

> **Face-to-face communication is preferred over simple memos.**

Why?

Because employees can:

- ask questions,
    
- express fears,
    
- get immediate clarification.
    

Example:

Instead of just sending:

> "New software will be implemented from Monday."

Management conducts meetings and explains:

- why it is needed,
    
- what changes,
    
- how employees will be trained.
    

---

## B. Participation Strategy

> **Involve employees in decisions.**

Instead of:

**Management → decision → employees**

use:

**Management + Employees → decision**

This increases ownership.

Example:

Workers are allowed to suggest how automation should be introduced on the factory floor.

---

## C. Support Strategy

Provide:

- **Training**
    
- **Counseling**
    
- **Coaching**
    

Example:

New software introduced → training sessions + mentor + counseling for employees who are anxious.
