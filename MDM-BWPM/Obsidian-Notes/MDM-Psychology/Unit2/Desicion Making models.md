## What is decision making?

Your PDF defines decision making as:

# What is the Rational Model?

The **Rational Model** assumes that you make a decision in a **logical, systematic and data-driven way**.

Your PDF gives these key words:

> **Stepwise, logical, data-driven, alternatives evaluation**.

So instead of saying:

> “I feel this option is better.”

you say:

> “Let me compare all the alternatives using evidence and choose the best one.”

---

# How does it work?

Think of this process:

Your PDF's broader decision-making process similarly includes identifying the problem, exploring alternatives, evaluating alternatives, choosing, implementing, and reviewing outcomes.

---

# Great example: Choosing a bridge design

Your PDF gives a civil-engineering example.

Suppose an engineer has **3 bridge designs**.

They don't simply choose:

> “Design A looks good.”

Instead they compare:

- **Cost**
    
- **Safety**
    
- **Durability**
    

They evaluate each design and then choose the best option.

For example:

|Design|Cost|Safety|Durability|
|---|---|---|---|
|A|Low|Medium|Medium|
|B|Medium|High|High|
|C|High|High|High|

The engineer systematically evaluates the alternatives.

That is **Rational Decision Making**.

---

# Another example: Buying a laptop

Suppose you want a laptop.

You have:

**Laptop A, B, C**

You compare:

- price
    
- processor
    
- RAM
    
- battery
    
- performance
    
- warranty
    

Then you choose based on the evidence.

You're not simply saying:

> “My friend has Laptop A, so I'll buy A.”

You're using a **structured evaluation**.

---

# Advantages

Your PDF gives two major advantages:

### 1. Clear and structured

There is a defined process.

You know:

> What problem am I solving?  
> What are my alternatives?  
> What criteria should I use?

### 2. Reduces mistakes

Because you systematically examine alternatives and evidence, you're less likely to make a careless decision.

---

# Disadvantages

But there's a catch.

Your PDF gives:

### 1. Time-consuming

Evaluating every alternative carefully takes time.

### 2. Requires full data

The model works best when sufficient information is available.

Imagine an emergency where you have **30 seconds** to decide.

You may not have time to gather complete data.

That's where other decision models become useful.

---

# When should you use Rational Model?

Use it when:

**Time is available + information is available + several alternatives need careful comparison.**

For example:

> Selecting a new CNC machine for a factory.

Your PDF gives this mechanical-engineering example, where efficiency, vendor reliability and maintenance cost are compared.

---

# Rational Model vs Intuitive Model

This distinction will become very important later.

### Rational

> **“Let me analyze everything.”**

### Intuitive

> **“I have experience; I can recognize the right choice quickly.”**

So:

**Rational → data + analysis**

**Intuitive → experience + gut feeling**

# Bounded Rationality

This is the **second decision-making model** in your PDF, after the Rational Model. Your PDF emphasizes **limited information and limited time**, along with **satisficing, time pressure and shortcuts**.

## What is Bounded Rationality?

The idea is:

> We want to make a rational decision, **but our ability to do so is limited**.

In real life, we usually don't have:

- unlimited time,
    
- complete information,
    
- unlimited resources,
    
- the ability to evaluate every possible alternative.
    

So instead of finding the **perfect/best** solution, we often choose a solution that is **good enough**.

That is called **satisficing**.

---

## The key idea

### Rational Model

> **“Find the best possible option.”**

### Bounded Rationality

> **“Find a satisfactory option within the available time and information.”**

This distinction is extremely important.

---

# Great example: Buying a laptop

Suppose you need a laptop **tomorrow**.

There are 100 different laptops available.

### Rational Model

You might:

This takes a lot of time.

### Bounded Rationality

You have only **2 hours**.

So you say:

> “I need 16 GB RAM, a decent processor, good battery and my budget is ₹70,000.”

You check 5–6 laptops.

You find one that satisfies your requirements.

> “Good enough. I'll take this.”

You **stop searching** even though there may be a slightly better laptop somewhere.

That's **bounded rationality**.

---

# Why is it called "bounded"?

Because rational decision-making is **bounded by limitations**.

---

# Engineering example from your PDF

Suppose your original cable supplier suddenly goes **out of stock**.

You need cables quickly.

Instead of doing a complete comparison of every supplier, you select an available supplier quickly based on availability.

Your PDF gives essentially this exact example.

You may not get the **best possible supplier**.

But you get one that is **acceptable and available now**.

That's bounded rationality.

---

# Another engineering example

Your PDF also gives an IT example:

A company needs to hire a programmer urgently.

There isn't enough time to fully test every candidate.

So they hire someone who is:

> **“good enough”**

rather than searching indefinitely for the absolute best candidate.

Again:

**Limited time → limited evaluation → satisfactory choice**

---

# Satisficing

This word is very important.

**Satisficing = satisfy + suffice**

It means choosing an option that is **good enough**, rather than trying to find the theoretically perfect option.

### Example

You need a hotel.

You don't compare 500 hotels.

You set:

> Price < ₹3,000  
> Rating > 4.0  
> Near college

The first hotel satisfying these requirements may be selected.

That's **satisficing**.

---

# Advantages

Your PDF gives:

### Quick

You can make decisions faster.

### Practical in the real world

Because real-world decisions often involve time and information constraints.

---

# Disadvantages

There is a trade-off.

Your PDF says bounded rationality:

> **May ignore better options and has risk of bias.**

Why?

Because you stop searching once you find something acceptable.

There may be a **better option**, but you never discover it.

---

# Rational vs Bounded Rationality

| Rational Model                        | Bounded Rationality       |
| ------------------------------------- | ------------------------- |
| Assumes extensive information         | Information is limited    |
| More time for analysis                | Time may be limited       |
| Evaluates alternatives systematically | Uses shortcuts            |
| Tries to find best option             | Finds satisfactory option |
| **Optimization**                      | **Satisficing**           |

### Memory trick

> **Rational = BEST**

> **Bounded Rationality = GOOD ENOUGH**

# Intuitive Decision-Making Model

This is the **third decision-making model** in your PDF, after Rational Model and Bounded Rationality. Your PDF describes it using the key ideas **gut feeling, experience-driven, and rapid choice**.

## What is Intuitive Decision Making?

**Intuitive decision making** means making a decision quickly based mainly on your **experience, knowledge, pattern recognition, and gut feeling**, rather than performing a long systematic analysis.

In simple words:

> **“I have seen this situation before, and something tells me what to do.”**

---

# Great example: Experienced mechanic

Imagine a mechanic hears a machine making a particular sound.

After only a few seconds, they say:

> “The bearing is going to fail.”

They haven't performed a complete diagnostic process yet.

Why can they make that decision?

Because they've encountered **similar machine sounds and failures many times before**.

Their brain recognizes the pattern quickly.

Your PDF gives a closely related example of an experienced technician recognizing from machine vibration that a bearing may fail soon.

That's **intuitive decision making**.

---

# Engineering example from your PDF

A civil engineer is supervising concrete work.

Suddenly, the weather changes and conditions become unsafe.

The site supervisor decides:

> **“Stop the concrete pouring.”**

They don't wait for a lengthy analysis because the situation requires a quick response.

Your PDF gives this as an example of the intuitive model: a site supervisor deciding to halt concrete pouring because of sudden weather changes without running full tests.

---

# How is this different from Rational Model?

### Rational

You think:

> “Let me collect the data, compare alternatives, and calculate the best option.”

### Intuitive

You think:

> “I've seen this before. I recognize what's happening.”

So:

**Rational = Analysis**

**Intuitive = Experience + Recognition**

---

# Why is intuition useful?

Your PDF says it is:

- **Fast**
    
- Useful when there is **uncertainty**
    
- Able to **leverage experience**
    

Imagine a machine is about to fail.

You don't have 2 hours to collect perfect data.

An experienced engineer may need to decide in seconds.

That's where intuition can be extremely valuable.

---

# But intuition has a weakness

Your PDF says it is:

> **Subjective and may overlook evidence.**

For example, an experienced engineer says:

> “This vibration is definitely a bearing problem.”

But maybe the actual problem is electrical.

If the engineer blindly trusts intuition and doesn't check the evidence, the decision can be wrong.

Intuitive decisions often rely heavily on this kind of rapid pattern recognition.

But this doesn't mean:

It's particularly useful when:

**1. Time is limited**

You need to decide quickly.

**2. The person has experience**

An expert can recognize patterns a beginner may not.

**3. The situation is uncertain**

There may not be enough complete data.

# Recognition-Primed Decision (RPD) Model

This is the **fourth and final decision-making model** in your PDF. It comes after the Rational, Bounded Rationality, and Intuitive models.

## What is RPD?

**Recognition-Primed Decision (RPD)** means making a decision by **recognizing a familiar pattern from previous experience** and then mentally evaluating what is likely to happen.

Your PDF highlights three key ideas:

> **Pattern recognition + expertise + simulation in mind**

### In simple words

> **“I've seen a situation like this before, so I recognize what is happening and know what usually works.”**

But there is one extra step compared with simple intuition:

**You recognize the situation → mentally simulate the possible action → decide.**

---

# Great example: Firefighter

Imagine an experienced firefighter enters a building.

They immediately notice:

- unusual smoke colour,
    
- heat pattern,
    
- location of smoke,
    
- sounds inside the building.
    

They think:

> “I've seen this pattern before. The fire is probably spreading behind this wall.”

They mentally imagine:

> “If we enter from this side, the fire could spread here.”

Then they choose the safest action.

That's the basic idea of **RPD**.

---

# Engineering example from your PDF

Suppose a senior electrician notices:

- a particular smell,
    
- a breaker pattern,
    
- symptoms that match an earlier incident.
    

They immediately recognize:

> “This looks like the short-circuit problem we dealt with before.”

Because of their experience, they rapidly identify the likely cause.

Your PDF gives this type of example for RPD.

---

So RPD works much better for **experts**.

# 1. Decision Matrix / Weighted Scoring Model

## What is it?

A **Decision Matrix** is a structured method used to compare multiple options using predefined criteria.

Your PDF says that:

- options are compared side-by-side,
- criteria are predefined,
- criteria receive weights according to importance,
- each option is scored,
- scores are multiplied by weights,
- totals help identify the best choice.

### Simple idea

Suppose you have to choose between:

**Goa, Mulshi, Alibag**

You can't simply say:

> “I like Goa.”

You decide using criteria:

- Duration
- Cost
- Fun activities
- Safety

Each criterion gets a **weight** based on importance.

For example, the PDF gives:

|Criterion|Weight|
|---|---|
|Duration|3|
|Cost|4|
|Fun activities|5|
|Safety|4|

So **fun activities** is considered more important than duration.

---

## How do you calculate it?

The PDF uses:

> **Rating × Weightage**

Then add all the values.

For example, for **Mulshi**:

The PDF's totals are:

- Goa = **51**
- Mulshi = **70**
- Alibag = **64**

Therefore:

> **Mulshi is selected because it has the highest weighted score.**

---

## Why is this useful?

It makes decision-making:

- logical,
- structured,
- easier to explain,
- less dependent on emotions.

### Memory trick

> **Decision Matrix = Weight × Rating → Total → Highest wins**

---

# 2. When to Use a Decision Matrix

The PDF gives four situations where a decision matrix is useful.

### 1. More than one good option

Example:

You have 3 good job offers.

A simple yes/no decision isn't enough.

### 2. Multiple factors matter

Maybe you need to consider:

**salary + location + growth + work culture**

You can assign different weights.

### 3. You want fair/objective comparison

Instead of:

> “I like Option A.”

you compare all options using the **same criteria**.

### 4. You want to reduce bias

A matrix can reduce emotional or rushed decisions by forcing you to use predefined criteria.

---

## When NOT to overcomplicate it

If the decision is extremely simple:

> “Tea or water?”

you don't need a decision matrix 😂.

It becomes useful when there are **multiple alternatives + multiple important criteria**.

# 4. SWOT Analysis

The PDF next introduces **SWOT Analysis** and uses it for decision-making exercises.

## What is SWOT?

**SWOT =**

**S — Strengths**  
**W — Weaknesses**  
**O — Opportunities**  
**T — Threats**

The easiest way to understand it is:

### Internal

**Strengths + Weaknesses**

These are things **inside the organization/project**.

### External

**Opportunities + Threats**

These come from the **outside environment**.

---

## Strengths

What are we good at?

Examples:

- strong R&D
- skilled employees
- good brand
- strong technology

Your PDF's product-launch example gives **R&D capability** as a strength.

---

## Weaknesses

Where are we lacking?

Examples:

- limited distribution
- insufficient staff
- high costs
- poor infrastructure

The PDF uses **limited distribution** as a weakness.

---

## Opportunities

What external condition can help us?

Examples:

- growing market
- new customer demand
- new technology
- new market segment

The PDF gives **growing market** as an opportunity.

---

## Threats

What external factor can hurt us?

Examples:

- competitors
- regulation
- economic downturn
- changing customer preferences

The PDF uses **competitors** as a threat.

---

# Great example

Imagine you're launching a new software product.

### Strength

Strong development team.

### Weakness

Small marketing team.

### Opportunity

Growing demand for AI tools.

### Threat

Large competitors already dominate the market.

Now you have a clearer picture of the situation and can make a better decision.

---

## Important exam point

Remember:

> **Strengths & Weaknesses = Internal**

> **Opportunities & Threats = External**

This is one of the easiest questions to ask in a fill-in-the-blank or match-the-pairs section.
